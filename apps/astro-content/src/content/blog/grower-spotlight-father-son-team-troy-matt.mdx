Allen sat down with father and son Troy and Matt Milford, poultry growers in Georgia, to talk ABF, family, and faith. Matt is a **fourth-generation** poultry grower. The land has been in the family since the late 1800s, when Troy's grandpa's uncle bought the farm. Grandpa got it and built three chicken houses.

Troy remembers spreading shavings for his grandpa — dumped out of a trailer at the end of the house, spread with a pitchfork. "It's changed a lot." Farming is what he wanted. He got married young, went and got an engineering degree (two-year from Gainesville), and was part-owner of an engineering company, director of sales and marketing, always wanting to be home.

He and his dad built two more houses in the early '90s, then two more around 2001. Eventually he told his wife it was time: "This chicken farmer's gonna make it." He quit the other work and came home.

Matt got out of high school, went to college a year or two, and didn't want to stay. "Only reason I went to college was because my mother." He convinced her to let him take a semester off. Troy's line: if you're going to farm, we have to build two more chicken houses. Now the farm supports the family — chickens and cattle, diversified. "We're living the dream. If you wanna call it that."

## ABF, extra eyes, and a moving target

The toughest challenge on the poultry side, Troy says, has been the antibiotic-free push — adjusting, finding other ways to keep birds healthy and converting like they should. You get something figured out, then something hits. Hatchery? Already on the farm? You have to react mid-flock.

That's why Matt on the place matters. "It's not just my eyes. It's another set of eyes. We can talk it over."

They also have family and friends growing nearby, flocks a couple of weeks older or younger. "We kind of know what's coming down the line… you can react a little quicker when you start seeing signs of it."

Before ABF, growers kept their cards close. After, more willingness to help — to an extent. "There's something you ain't gonna tell." It's still competition. In engineering, Troy said, there were things people looked at, tried, and couldn't copy because of a little secret you weren't going to tell. Same here.

On a six-house farm, average or above, top five, he's tickled. You don't get the same birds across six houses. Two or three mediocre, two or three real good, maybe one that bites you. They use Southland products, play with how they're running, change it when a flock says this isn't working.

An older man told them the best thing you can put on your farm is your shadow. Pay attention. Live in the two weeks in front of you.

Troy learned to figure stuff out from his dad and grandpa — money isn't always there, so you engineer it. They put in their own controllers. "When you start having to pay a fee when you come fix everything, it gets expensive."

## How a father and son keep working together

They fight. Matt said you can ask his mom and his wife. Half the time it's about how they're doing something. Five, ten minutes later, they're back. "You can't let little stuff get in the way of something like what we do."

If it wasn't something he loved, Matt would not have built two new chicken houses. "It's a commitment. That ties me down financially for years. But it's something I love, and it's something I'm willing to work for."

Allen asked Troy how he balanced family so a son didn't despise the work.

> God. What makes our family what our family is, is our faith in God, and our trust in Him. Me and Matt try to start every day in our Bible and doing devotion. If it wasn't for our faith in God, I don't believe we could work together… There ain't no way it could work without God.

There was a time it was Troy, his dad, and his grandpa. "What a privilege to be able to spend time together." Now it's him, his dad, and his son. Matt has a little boy. They hope one day he wants to do what they do.

> Everybody can't say I get to go to work every day with my family. To lay your head down on your pillow at night and be at peace, and know that we still love each other and can still talk to each other. That means a lot.
