Necrotic enteritis is not always the result of a single problem. In many cases, it develops when multiple stressors affect a bird’s intestinal health at the same time. Coccidiosis can damage the intestinal lining, creating conditions that allow Clostridium bacteria to multiply and produce toxins. Together, these challenges can put significant pressure on a bird’s gut health.

Let's take a closer look at how these factors work together, why gut resilience matters, and what poultry growers can do to help reduce the risk of necrotic enteritis.

## Necrotic Enteritis Starts With Stacked Stressors

One way to understand necrotic enteritis is to think about the cumulative effect of stressors. A single challenge may not cause a major problem on its own, but several challenges occurring together can overwhelm a bird’s ability to cope.

Coccidiosis is often the starting point. When coccidia damage the intestinal lining, the bird’s ability to absorb nutrients and maintain its protective gut barrier can be compromised. This damage can contribute to inflammation, increased mucus production, changes in the intestinal microbiome, and reduced nutrient absorption.

As the intestinal lining becomes less effective, more undigested material can remain in the gut. This creates an opportunity for other problems to develop.

## How Clostridium Contributes to Necrotic Enteritis

Clostridium bacteria are common in poultry environments, including poultry houses, litter floors, and the surrounding farm environment. Their presence alone does not mean a flock will develop necrotic enteritis. Under normal conditions, the gut microbiome helps keep these bacteria in balance.

However, when coccidiosis damages the intestine and digestion becomes less efficient, undigested protein can accumulate in the gut. This material provides a food source for Clostridium, allowing the bacteria to multiply.

As Clostridium proliferates, it can produce toxins that damage the intestinal lining and contribute to necrotic enteritis. In other words, coccidiosis can create the conditions that make a bird more susceptible, while an increase in Clostridium can further compound the damage.

## Building Gut Resilience

The presence of coccidia or Clostridium does not make necrotic enteritis inevitable. The goal is to support the bird's ability to withstand these challenges by reducing bacterial pressure and promoting healthy digestion and nutrient absorption.

There are two areas growers can focus on:

### 1. Reduce Bacterial Pressure Through Biosecurity

Biosecurity practices help limit the bacterial load in the poultry house and surrounding environment. Consistent cleaning and disinfection of feed lines, water lines, and equipment, along with proper litter management, can help reduce opportunities for harmful bacteria to build up.

During periods of increased disease pressure, growers may also review their sanitation practices and consider additional measures, such as fogging appropriate disinfectants. Any disinfectant used should be suitable for the intended application and handled according to its label directions.

### 2. Support Digestion and Nutrient Absorption

Growers may have limited control over feed formulation, particularly in integrated operations, but there are other ways to support gut health.

Probiotics can help promote a healthy balance of beneficial bacteria in the intestinal microbiome. Organic acids may also support intestinal health and digestion, helping birds make better use of nutrients.

Supporting beneficial bacteria and healthy digestion can help limit the conditions that allow Clostridium to proliferate.

## A Proactive Approach to Poultry Health

Necrotic enteritis can be a complicated challenge because it often involves several factors working together. While growers cannot eliminate every pathogen or environmental stressor, they can take steps to reduce bacterial pressure and support intestinal health.

Consistent biosecurity, proper litter management, and attention to gut health all play a role in building resilience. Understanding how coccidiosis, Clostridium, and other stressors interact can help growers make more informed decisions when managing flock health.
