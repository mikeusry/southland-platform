Bryant Madren is a pastor who has also been growing poultry for six years. You may have seen him on his wife's YouTube channel, [The Farming Pastor's Wife](https://www.youtube.com/@TheFarmingPastorsWife "The Farming Pastor's Wife"). In this video, Bryant takes the stage to share two major hacks he uses on his poultry farm.

"I spent about $2 million here on my farm getting it the way that it is," he told Allen. "Anything I can do to protect my investment, or to make my investment more valuable if I were to liquidate it down the road, it's always the best option for me."

## 1. Jig for Laying Drip Lines

When Bryant first built his houses in 2016, he didn't implement any drip lines or erosion control. Water coming off the grade at the end of house one and house six cost him **$17,000** to re-grade and put a little rock down the drip line. "That cost me because of my lack of experience."

Since then, Bryant's philosophy has been: "Do it right the first time, and do it right as soon as you can." Once he was eligible for a [USDA NRCS](https://www.nrcs.usda.gov/ 'USDA NRCS') (Natural Resources Conservation Service) grant for erosion control, he used it — and built a jig to spread the rock.

Erosion isn't only cosmetic. Water can seek back under the foundation and create challenges inside the house. Once that seepage starts, it's hard to correct.

NRCS wanted 12 inches of coverage on both sides of the drip line. Bryant's rock comes out about **30 inches** from the wall, **five inches** thick instead of the required four. He didn't want them measuring low spots, and rock settles. A little buffer.

He made the jig because dumping a bucket and raking by hand is fine if you've got seven, eight, nine, ten people. On a one-, two-, or three-man show, it isn't. He thought of it like a box blade: gravel in the front, even out the back. Five-inch C channel on the bottom, a four-inch C channel welded as a wiper. Dump the rock, pull up, 30 inches wide, five inches thick.

He also wanted landscaping fabric underneath. He added a roller on the front of the jig. His wife drove the tractor; he and his son ran two skid steers — drop the rock, she pulls up, fabric rolls off, weight of the rock holds it.

His NRCS guy, Randy Blackwood, encouraged him to patent it. Bryant was toying with that idea when they filmed. "Poultry farmers around the country could benefit from it."

## 2. Bryant's Compost Shed

His integrator contract requires a way to dispose of dead birds: incinerator or compost. An incinerator costs money every time you run it. Natural decomposition is free — a few dollars of shavings and litter to get the temperature right.

NRCS grant money for a shed like this typically waits until you've been growing three years. The contract still required a mortality plan on day one. Inspired by his nephew's stack house, Bryant bought two 20×20 carports ($1,500 apiece) and built four compost bins underneath them with his brother, brother-in-law, and a couple of nephews in one Saturday. About **$5,500–$6,000** in 2016. He used that setup five to six years.

In North Carolina, you cannot leave litter outside unsheltered more than **14 days**. The compost side worked. The litter side didn't — sell birds in January and you're hard pressed to get it on the field. He had to cover it and wait for dry weather. "That's a bear."

Once he qualified for the NRCS grant, he built the facility in the video: about **42–44 feet wide** including the apron, **180 feet** to the ends of the concrete, roughly 160–165 feet inside. Litter and birds. He has a small cow-calf operation — about **50 mama cows**, and he bales about **700–750 rolls** a year — so he needs that litter for forage. Birds went out the day before they filmed; they were in the middle of crusting.

A young man he knows with an incinerator spends about **$1,500–$2,000 per flock**. Five flocks a year is about $10,000 a year. Over five years that's about **$50,000** in diesel. Bryant spent about $6,000 to start and waited for the grant.
