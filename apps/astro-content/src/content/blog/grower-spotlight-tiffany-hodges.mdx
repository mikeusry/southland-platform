Tiffany Hodges grows poultry in Guntersville, Alabama, with her husband Aubrey. They also run a wedding venue. She grew up in the city. No farm experience.

Aubrey's dad had six houses when they got together. His dad had foot surgery and some pretty high losses — this was before products like [Big Ole Bird](/products/poultry-probiotic/). Tiffany's first time in a chicken house, she picked up several hundred birds, close to a thousand.

"Shortly after that, we got engaged because I stuck around through all the chickens." They've been married almost ten years. Two boys in 21 months. Chicken houses, row crop, farm wife, mom — "a lot of different roles to fill, depending on the day."

## A woman in a male-dominated field

As a woman in agriculture, she said, you deal with sexism and the assumption you don't know as much as your male counterpart.

> The best advice I can give women that deal with that is just to stand strong in what you believe in, and deal with people that will treat you with respect. You have to kind of learn to balance it in a male-dominated role, and show them that you know what you're doing, and then you can earn their respect — as much as you hate to say that you have to do that sometimes.

She feels it on both fronts: farmer, and businesswoman running the wedding venue. "Glad I got a good one that knows I'm an equal."

## How they still like each other

Friends ask how, after ten years of marriage, they still like each other so much when they work together — sometimes together more than not.

"With anything, you truly have to enjoy what you do. We love working on the farm, and raising our boys here, and they love the farm life — every aspect from row crop to chicken houses to running around in muddy puddles. You just have to make it work, and work together as a unit, and sometimes you have to give each other a little extra grace."

Aubrey, she said, baptized her by fire. Short lessons. "He says here, have at it." They've made it this far, still pretty happily married.

## What she will and won't run

The line she's drawn: the International. She will not haul grain. She will pick, run grain cart, pick up chickens, bush-hog, corn stalks, zero-turn. Pretty much everything else.

"I'm the smallest one, so I'm the one that always has to help work on things, because I've literally had to lay up on corn heads, and dig out ant beds." Put the rod in there and let you fix it. Yep.
