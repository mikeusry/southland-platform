## More Uses for Pure B.S.

Pure B.S. is a concentrated bio surfactant for recurring organic buildup in and around the home. It is a cleaner, not a disinfectant or sanitizer.

**Floors and utility areas.** Mix 4 oz of Pure B.S. with 1 gallon of clean water. Mop, scrub, or foam-clean sealed hard floors and utility areas as needed.

**Kitchens, bathrooms, and hard surfaces.** Add 5 oz of Pure B.S. to a 32 oz spray bottle and fill with clean water. Spray the surface, allow a few minutes to soak, then wipe clean. For heavy grease or buildup, allow longer contact time and agitate before wiping or rinsing. Test in an inconspicuous area first, and rinse food-contact surfaces with clean water after use.

## The Fly Is Not the Problem

Almost everything sold for drain flies targets the adult insect. That is the wrong target. Adults live about two weeks and are weak fliers that rarely leave the room they hatched in. Going after them changes nothing about the drain, and the drain is where the next generation is already developing.

A female drain fly lays up to 300 eggs in 48 hours. Larvae hatch in about two days and mature into adults in as little as eight. That is why a drain nobody thought about can go from invisible to a visible swarm in roughly two weeks — and why a spray that "worked" on Monday is followed by fresh flies by the weekend.

## Remove the Food Source

Drain fly larvae eat biofilm. Take the biofilm away and there is nothing to eat, nowhere to lay eggs, and no next generation. That is the entire strategy, and it is the only one that holds.

The difficulty is that biofilm is genuinely hard to remove. It is not loose debris sitting in the trap — it is a structured matrix of bacteria, polysaccharides, and lipids adhered to the pipe wall, often well down the run of the pipe where no brush reaches. Hot water runs over it. Bleach rinses past it. Caustic openers scour some of it away and leave a surface that regrows the film.

A bio surfactant works on the bond between the film and the pipe. Pure B.S. is made with surfactin produced by *Bacillus subtilis*, and it helps wet, lift, and release that film so it washes away in normal flow. Weekly treatment for a month keeps removing it while the eggs already in the pipe work through their cycle.

## Treat the Right Drain

The most common reason a treatment appears to fail is that it was applied to the wrong drain. Drain flies stay close to where they hatched, but "the bathroom" can mean the sink, the shower, the floor drain, or the overflow.

Dry each suspect drain, then tape a piece of clear packing tape across the opening overnight, leaving a small gap for airflow. In the morning, the drain producing the flies will have them stuck to the tape. Treat that one — and, since biofilm rarely grows in only one place, treat the others while you are at it.

If you have treated every drain and flies keep appearing, check the sources people forget: the washing machine drain pan, the dishwasher air gap, the refrigerator drip tray, the HVAC condensate line, and the basement sump pit.

## Pour or Foam?

For a household drain, pour. The overnight soak holds product in the P-trap, and the trap and low points stay wet continuously, which is why treatment placed there keeps working overnight. The limit is the vertical tailpiece above the trap water line and the sink overflow channel — a pour rinses those for seconds, not hours.

Foam is the professional answer to that gap, because it expands into a space and clings to vertical wall. Virginia Tech Extension recommends a microbial foam for drain fly larvae for exactly that reason. We don't sell a foaming applicator and won't send you to a consumer one — foam that collapses in a minute is worse than a good pour, because it feels like you did more.

The free version of the same fix: pour at night, then take a stiff bottle brush to the tailpiece and overflow. Full walkthrough in [how to get rid of drain flies](/blog/how-to-get-rid-of-drain-flies/).
